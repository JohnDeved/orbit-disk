# Orbit Disk: scan and search design

## Resumable mapping

Each location has a private SQLite staging database. Directory listings and traversal progress are committed as mapping proceeds, and the partial map is available for browsing, search and eligible removal. Switching locations leaves mapping running; stopping or reopening the app resumes from committed progress. An interrupted listing may need to be read again, and an incompatible filesystem identity or event history can require reconciliation or a new baseline. An existing saved map remains available while a replacement is built.

If a mapping worker fails, returning to its location or a folder within it opens the committed stage again instead of reusing the failed task. Focused model checks cover failures while visible and in the background, force refresh, a retained saved map, and navigation overlapping failure feedback.

Resuming a stage batches removal-history headers, yielding between unrelated batches. Once a relevant operation is found, its remaining header batch uses the existing page-by-page replay, with fresh completion and liveness checks. Root binding is checked throughout replay; the durable cursor advances only after reconciliation. An already acknowledged history avoids the additional replay-only validation.

In eight alternating warm APFS trials with 5,000 synthetic unrelated history entries and a concurrent journal writer, median first view fell from 5.29 to 1.48 seconds. The writer's pooled 95th-percentile begin-and-complete time fell from 384 to 40 ms. Twelve trials with 1,000 relevant entries improved first view from 1.09 to 0.70 seconds, with writer p95 of 287 versus 233 ms. After removing idle-path overhead, twenty empty-history trials measured 7.31 versus 7.37 ms; two further history trials per variant retained the gains. These are core-only warm-cache measurements. Writer samples within each trial are correlated, and individual filesystem calls can exceed the batch budget; this does not establish cold-disk, whole-Mac or rendered-input latency.

The stage retains the scan-start filesystem event checkpoint. When traversal finishes, the app seals the staged snapshot, checkpoints its SQLite journal, clones the database and atomically publishes the clone. It then replays filesystem changes; a crash cannot expose a partially published replacement. The stage is kept separately from the purgeable saved-map cache. The app excludes both locations from scans and change monitoring.

The durable pipeline trades throughput for recoverable progress. In an isolated warm APFS fixture of 10,000 small files, an earlier in-memory scan and save reached its first model-visible listing in 48–51 ms and finished in 97–99 ms; the initial staged implementation took 51–61 ms and 273–279 ms, respectively. Peak model-process RSS was about 45 MB before and 54–56 MB with staging. A subsequent write-path change reduced a separate staged 10,000-file core scan and publication from 223 ms to 197–200 ms in two alternating runs, without changing transaction boundaries or durability. The measurements use generated files, exclude rendered input-to-display latency, and do not predict cold or whole-Mac performance.

In a later comparison of the completed model with an earlier staged revision, four alternating fresh-process runs over 10,000 warm one-byte files gave a median first visible listing of 53.5 ms versus 47.3 ms, and completion of 290.1 ms versus 317.8 ms. Median peak RSS was 52.1 MiB versus 51.6 MiB. These tests polled model state at 1 ms intervals and did not render the interface; the small sample does not establish whole-Mac or cold-disk performance.

In a separate core-only comparison on 100,000 warm files, the staged scanner's median completion was 4.17 s with about 117 MiB peak RSS, versus 0.83 s and about 174 MiB for the old in-memory scan and save. The staged scanner reported its first progress at about 18 ms. Most staged time was in durable listing transactions; increasing the WAL checkpoint threshold did not improve the test, while larger batches increased memory and the amount of work repeated after interruption. Those settings were left unchanged. The numbers exclude the app model and rendering.

Fresh stages now reuse the existing file-identity index for hard-link owner rebuilding instead of maintaining a second index over the same identities. Three alternating warm 100,000-file core scans had a median completion of 3.243 s versus 4.172 s with the extra index; first-progress times and peak RSS overlapped. Existing stages retain their old index when resumed, avoiding an index drop during reopening. Durability and commit boundaries are unchanged. These are synthetic core timings, not whole-Mac or rendered-interface measurements.

Directory registration reuses its SQLite statements within each listing batch while retaining the identity check and transaction boundaries. In four interleaved runs inserting 20,000 synthetic sibling-folder records, median insertion time fell from 303 to 240 ms. A warm scan of 3,000 real empty folders varied too much to establish an end-to-end improvement. The staged checks cover repeated identities, conflicting identities and rollback, as well as interruption and publication recovery.

The pending-directory and dirty queues use partial SQLite indexes, created transactionally on the first advance after the partial map is attached. In two interleaved warm scans of 10,000 empty sibling folders, the deferred-index implementation completed in 2.60–2.72 seconds versus 3.58–4.05 seconds before; another compilation was running during part of the comparison. An earlier eager-index prototype showed no clear difference on two 100,000-file scans. In one resumed-stage run with deferred creation, the first partial root view took 133 ms before and 134 ms after, while the remaining scan fell from 2.33 to 1.59 seconds. A held WAL reader, cancellation during creation and a subprocess killed between the two index creations were checked; rollback preserved the old records and retry succeeded. These are synthetic local timings, not cold-disk or rendered-frame measurements.

Each listing now creates its epoch in the first batch transaction, or in the completion transaction for an empty directory. This removes one durable commit per directory while retaining SQLite FULL synchronization and the existing batch size. The active directory remains visible as mapping before its first commit. Tests kill a subprocess before and after the first batch and empty-listing commits, then resume; they also cover rollback, empty fallback, hard links, deletion replay and stale completion rejection.

In six alternating runs through the actual app model with fresh indexes and warm generated files, mapping 2,000 empty folders took a median 709 ms before and 637 ms after this change (ranges 651–774 and 599–701 ms). The median main-loop polling gap remained 6.4 ms. For 128 folders containing 128 files each, completion changed from 410 to 387 ms and the first visible model results remained at 54 ms. One-file-per-folder and flat-folder timings overlapped substantially. These measurements include model preparation but do not measure rendered frames or cold whole-Mac scans.

## Performance work

Collection requests belong to their mapping session and queue while earlier requests or removals run. Accepted drops reserve their source session and token while macOS delivers the data, so a later drag or location switch cannot replace them. Gesture eligibility uses metadata already displayed; filesystem policy and identity checks run on a worker, including single-file drops. Large requests validate in rotating slices of at most 128 items; the first slice is published promptly and later slices are combined into increasing batches capped at 8,192 items. Buffered items are rechecked in rotating slices before publication, with rechecks restarted after collection edits or removals. Review and deletion remain available for validated entries while additions are pending. A deletion captures its reviewed collection; pending additions revalidate afterward. Clear Collection cancels pending additions and reserved drops so they cannot refill the tray.

In a single synthetic preparation-only run over 100,000 distinct entries, the earlier one-batch approach took 363 ms; the current capped publication schedule took 2.73 seconds across 18 commits. Both produced the same collection. Intermediate commits make validated items available sooner and let smaller requests proceed, at the cost of additional total preparation. This comparison excludes filesystem validation, queue scheduling and rendering; it does not predict whole-app latency.

Location resolution checks existing ancestor stages and saved maps off the main actor. A stopped parent can resume when a child is opened, and a fresh app can discover the parent's durable work. Saved targets must match their current identities; a pending stage can still discover an unlisted child. Each session retains its last prepared folder and selection for immediate return, guarded by its root identity and removal revision. Opening a folder prioritizes its pending ancestors and descendants after deletion repair work.

Parent mappings can read a completed descendant map instead of enumerating those directories again. Reuse streams bounded pages through the existing durable listing transactions and recomputes hard-link ownership for the parent. The source must have compatible volume change history and no unresolved removal records. The parent persists the earlier event checkpoint before copying records, so interruption cannot lose the history needed to reconcile imported data. Changed, incomplete or incompatible sources fall back to filesystem enumeration; explicit full refresh also enumerates again.

After accepting a source, reuse checks at most 32 immediate children before choosing its path. Smaller listings use native bulk enumeration, which avoids repeated removal-journal setup; the accepted source and its durable event checkpoint remain available for larger descendants. The initial source directory can still be copied when small so a deeply nested large folder can benefit. Copied pages use the existing 512-record write limit, with a cancellable 2 ms gap after full pages to let removal operations acquire the released journal fence.

In two alternating fresh-process pairs on 100,000 generated empty APFS files with warm caches, durable traversal took a median 4.19 s through filesystem enumeration and 1.77 s through the selected reuse path. Three alternating pairs over 1,000 directories with three files each took about 0.580 s through either path; copying every small listing had been slower and was not retained. These measurements include stage creation and durable traversal, but exclude fixture setup, final publication, event-stream inspection and UI work. A separate concurrent removal of one generated file began its durable intent in 2.8 ms and completed in 5.6 ms; the interrupted import reconciled and saved the correct remaining files. That single action is not a latency bound, and these warm local fixtures do not predict a cold whole-Mac scan.

Mounted-volume discovery runs off the main actor, with overlapping requests coalesced and stale results discarded. The Mac root stays selectable while its capacity is loading; a failed lookup does not invent a size. In a gated model test with discovery held, initialization returned in 1.09 ms and the interface could continue handling requests. Local discovery was already fast on this Mac, with a 1.83 ms first-query median across 15 fresh processes. A hung query can delay later device-list updates, but it no longer blocks the interface.

Filesystem event batches are normalized in order on a serial worker, rather than resolving their paths on the main actor. The pending queue is bounded; overflow triggers a conservative refresh while known cache-only events remain excluded. A local test enqueued 10,000 synthetic events on the main actor in 0.02–0.05 ms, and merging 10,000 excluded events into an overflow took 1.50–1.61 ms. Tests cover real create, rename and delete events, Home/Data volume aliases, exclusion bursts, root replacement, ordering and stopped monitors. The figures measure dispatch work, not full refresh latency.

In an earlier implementation, large grouped drags installed the completed collection as one snapshot. In a synthetic 100,000-item drag, start fell from 3.2–4.7 seconds to 0.04–0.06 ms and drop dispatch from 2.1–2.9 seconds to 1.55–2.30 ms. Preparing 100,000 already validated items took 898–1,219 ms off-main, followed by 0.26–14.87 ms on the main actor, compared with 1.27–1.67 seconds on the actor before. These historical timings exclude filesystem validation, rendered frames and physical pointer input; they do not measure the current queued batching.

Collection snapshots retain their membership set and total size, so repeated reads do not rebuild them on the main actor. In three alternating model runs with 100,000 collected items, median single-item drag preparation fell from about 46 ms to 0.024 ms and group preparation from 44.6 ms to 0.048 ms; peak RSS rose by roughly 4–5 MiB. Adding one item to a collection over 1,000 items now uses the cancellable worker too. In three alternating synthetic 100,000-item model runs, median drop dispatch fell from 1,007 ms to 0.043 ms; end-to-end preparation still took roughly 1.0–1.05 s on the worker, and peak RSS rose about 8–10 MiB. These timings exclude rendering and physical pointer input.

Removing rows from a large collection also rebuilds the snapshot on a worker. Pending rows are indicated in place and destructive actions wait until the edits are applied. In three alternating model runs with 100,000 collected items, dispatch fell from 422–529 ms to 0.035–0.047 ms; the worker took 407–410 ms, the final main-actor callback 13.4–14.0 ms, and peak RSS rose by about 10 MiB. The offscreen layout checks include pending and disabled states, but the timings do not measure rendered frames or physical input.

Collection coverage stops walking a parent chain once that ancestor is already known. In three interleaved model runs removing one item from 100,000 synthetic sibling files, completion fell from 417–445 ms to 162–165 ms while dispatch remained below 0.07 ms. A separate 30,000-case comparison checked the exact stored byte-key sets and query results, including Unicode-equivalent but byte-distinct paths. These are synthetic model timings, not rendered interaction measurements.

Removal startup reserves the original root and identity while publishing activity. If an observer switches locations or forces a refresh during a synchronous notification, the original session retains its in-flight removal without transferring its status to the newly visible location. Focused checks exercise switches at both notification boundaries, refresh and duplicate-removal attempts, root invalidation, and newer feedback; they verify that the captured files still belong to the original root.

Removal completion prepares the remaining collection and removal overlay off the main actor, then checks the session and collection revision before installing them. In three real-model runs with one gated deletion and 100,000 synthetic retained rows, the first queued main-thread callback was delayed about 165 ms before this change and 0.04–0.06 ms afterward. A later 1 ms timer observed a 14–37 ms gap in the synthetic completion path, so the measurements do not establish consistently smooth rendered frames. Tests cover concurrent collection edits, location switches, refresh, nested removals and monitoring failures.

Collection validation belongs to its mapping session, so an accepted collection action can finish when another location is open or mapping is paused. Results and feedback remain with the original session. The tray and graph show only the displayed location's collection, and review and confirmation actions carry session, root and collection-revision tokens to reject stale actions after a switch. Tests cover offscreen completion, same-root refresh, reentrant navigation and stale review callbacks.

Saving an index checks ordinary parent paths directly, with Foundation retained for unusual path forms. It keeps the same atomic transaction and publication. In interleaved synthetic saves of 150,000 nodes, three saves per process used about 104.4–104.7 billion retired instructions before and 93.7–94.2 billion after; elapsed times overlapped under system load. Path-parity checks included Unicode, relative paths, repeated separators and dot components; a malformed tree must leave the previous snapshot intact. These results do not predict the save time for a whole Mac.

In the earlier in-memory scanner, a wide first-scan listing published a bounded batch of 512 pending entries before enumeration finished. Those entries could be browsed but not collected until confirmed. In an offscreen model and layout test of a warm synthetic 50,002-file folder, median time to the first realized row fell from 914 ms to 337 ms across six scans per variant. Median scan completion was 1,761 ms before and 1,723 ms after in that sample; CPU time and peak memory were modestly higher. The current resumable scanner uses a different publication path; these historical measurements are not a cold whole-Mac measurement or a physical mouse-to-display test.

Search retains the current results and any selected or hovered previous result, rather than every result seen during the session. In a synthetic 260,000-entry index, 100 distinct 300-result searches raised physical footprint from 6.5 to 41.7 MiB before the change; the lookup remained at 41.7 MiB after clearing search. The revised lookup held the process at 7.7 MiB in the same test. These are long-session model measurements, not app rendering measurements.

Incremental refresh collects changed file identities in SQLite and recalculates hard-link ownership in batches. It updates ancestor totals only where allocation actually changes, in addition to the replaced folders' ancestors. In two alternating batches of four warm 20,000-file synthetic folder replacements, the median update time fell from 639.9 ms to 322.2 ms; totals were checked after every update. The measurement includes index replacement and publication, but not filesystem enumeration or whole-Mac refreshes. Tests cover cross-folder hard links, nested ancestor totals and interrupted updates preserving the previous snapshot.

Refresh planning reuses immutable indexed metadata for repeated ancestor paths, while still checking the filesystem for each path, and removes covered paths by walking ancestors rather than comparing every pair. In four interleaved process runs of five warm trials over 500 synthetic changed folders, the median of run medians fell from about 219 ms to 73 ms; the planned paths matched. Cancellation, stale snapshots, overlapping names and replaced symlinks are checked separately. SQLite refresh also computes each affected parent's totals in one pass and looks up changed file identities directly. Warm synthetic one-file updates in a 100,000-file folder took 247–276 ms before and 208–222 ms after across interleaved process medians. These timings exclude filesystem scanning and UI work.

Index updates validate replacement paths with a set and ancestor walk instead of comparing every pair. In a local calculation test of 10,000 distinct sibling paths, validation took about 1.1 ms rather than 4.0 s. Randomized path comparisons and the core checks cover duplicates, nested paths, Unicode equivalence, cancellation and preservation of the saved snapshot on rejection. This is validation time only, not the duration of a filesystem refresh or database update.

Indexed folder hydration reuses its descendant query rather than preparing it once per child directory. Four interleaved process runs on a warm synthetic index with 20,000 empty directories reduced the median of run medians from 372 to 251 ms; empty and nested descendants are covered by a regression check. This measures data loading, not rendered navigation.

Graph hit testing uses a grid of candidate paths, retaining original path order and exact containment checks. In an optimized 768-sector synthetic test, 15,000 pointer lookups took 279–350 ms before and 6.2–7.1 ms after, with matching results. Candidate bounds are padded to account for SwiftUI path-bound rounding. Interaction checks compare against direct path containment at boundaries, gaps and overlapping rings. These are lookup timings, not input-to-display latency.

For lists of at least 1,000 items, graph hover can use a row-ID lookup cached for the displayed ordering. Navigation, sorting and result changes invalidate it; large related groups still use a linear pass when that is cheaper. A synthetic 100,000-row test measured 3,000 near-end lookups at 8.06 seconds before and 0.00035 seconds after, plus 0.0059 seconds to build the cache. The result covers lookup work, not rendering or scroll latency.

Large saved and progressive folder views prepare row IDs, lookup tables, and color assignments away from the main actor; obsolete preparations and index reads are cancelled on navigation. In an isolated synthetic 100,000-file model test, main-actor installation and first row access fell from 194.65 to 25.73 ms for a fresh folder, and from 169.42 to 15.79 ms for a same-folder refresh. Preparation still takes time on a worker; these figures exclude full rendering and input-to-display latency. Large name sorts also run on a cancellable worker while the current list remains usable, and the related graph hover is revealed again when ordering changes.

Prepared folder colors now track the revision of the color mapping separately from the graph presentation revision. A same-folder publication can reuse prepared colors when an intervening publication did not change the mapping; additions still merge with the latest mapping. In an isolated 100,000-row model test of that stale-presentation case, main-actor installation fell from 24.1–24.5 ms to 10.3–15.4 ms. The normal path was about 8–11 ms. These are synthetic installation timings, not rendered-frame measurements, and the frequency of the stale-presentation case in normal use is unknown.

For a changed order of at least 5,000 rows, the native list is recreated instead of asking AppKit to move each row. The visible row and its pixel offset, selection, focus and graph hover are restored, unless newer user input supersedes the restoration. Sorting and same-folder refresh publication wait for an active drag to finish. In one paired offscreen test with 100,000 synthetic rows in reverse name order, a correctly ordered visible row appeared after 16,079 ms before this change and 160 ms after; the previous visible anchor was restored by 186 ms. This is one synthetic run, not a guarantee for other folders or devices.

Graph preparation reuses direct-child path IDs already produced for the current folder. Nine synthetic preparations of a 100,000-child graph took 52–61 ms before and 32–39 ms after; geometry, colors and nested hover membership matched. Navigation and refresh invalidate the IDs. These timings measure graph preparation on this Mac, not frame rate.

When a large folder forms one whole-ring group, the graph reuses the folder's existing child array, ordered IDs and membership set. It avoids copying all members and building another member-to-segment dictionary. Mixed and nested graphs retain their existing construction; zero-sized entries, ambiguous IDs and overflowing totals use that path too. Tests compare exact arc geometry, member bytes and order, lookup, collection states, and publication during navigation, search, sorting and held drags. In three alternating offscreen hosted-view runs with 100,000 synthetic files, median view refresh after model installation changed from 97.4 to 68.3 ms; median peak resident memory changed from 327.9 to 306.8 MB. Mixed and nested controls showed no clear improvement or persistent regression. These small-sample measurements exclude disk scanning and physical input latency.

Collection marks for a large graph group now check the typically smaller set of collected paths against its existing membership set. Groups with duplicate or canonically equivalent names fall back to the original byte-exact scan. In an offscreen 100,000-member test, a collection change through raster capture took 64–81 ms before and 0.88–2.32 ms after, with the changed pixels verified. An additional 8,192 coverage combinations check the optimized result against the original method. These results do not measure physical mouse-to-display latency.

Search skips its second index scan when the exact match count is zero or the requested result limit is zero. On a warm synthetic one-million-entry index, five absent searches had a median of 78.3 ms before and 38.2 ms after, excluding the app's debounce. Selective substring indexes were prototyped but are not included: they increase storage and indexing work, and their availability on every supported macOS version has not been established.

When searching a partial map without pending removals, matching rows smaller than the retained result threshold are counted and validated without constructing file objects. Equal-size matches retain the original path ordering, and searches with removal overlays use the original projection. On a warm staged fixture with 20,000 varied-size matching files, core searches took 26.9–28.3 ms before and 10.6–11.7 ms after; two alternating model runs including the 100 ms debounce took 126.4–135.4 ms before and 112.5–125.5 ms after. A 10,000-file equal-size fixture showed no speedup. Tests compare an independent folder traversal, limits, ties, Unicode filenames and overlays; they also cover damaged rows, concurrent mapping, and cancellation during SQLite execution. These synthetic timings do not measure rendered frames or cold storage.

Equal-size candidates that cannot outrank the retained heap now skip node construction too, while comparing the same Foundation-derived path bytes. In four alternating processes on a warm 20,000-file equal-size fixture, median core time fell from 47.94 ms to 42.30 ms. Model medians including the debounce were 149.65 ms before and 144.84 ms after, with overlapping individual timings; a varied-size core sample was 10.72 ms before and 10.91 ms after. A regression fixture includes normalized-equal paths with different identities and verifies the same results through the unchanged removal-overlay path. These are synthetic measurements and exclude rendered frames.
When a disk model is released, it cancels its own search task and token without cancelling mapping sessions. In a gated 3,000-file staged-map test, the old search continued to completion after the model was released; the revised worker exited with CancellationError and emitted no later model notification. The test stops the separate journal monitor first, since other active work can retain the model and delay its release.

Live preview records each child path once per directory listing. Superseded preview reads check for cancellation while gathering and hydrating entries and before caching an ordering. The ordinary nonthrowing sort remains uninterrupted to avoid slowing normal first previews; cancellation during that sort waits for it to finish. Warm synthetic scans showed a small, variable improvement; they do not measure cold storage or whole-home mapping.

## Hover responsiveness (0.9.1)

Hover no longer dims unrelated branches. Crossing a sector gap therefore leaves every branch color unchanged. The colored Canvas and collection marks sit in an equatable base layer keyed by presentation revision and metrics; a separate Canvas draws at most the hovered and selected outlines. Collection, folder-publication and geometry changes invalidate the appropriate layer.

Native hit testing caches the exact sector paths until the root, layout revision or metrics change. Each mouse-move event resolves its segment once for both the cursor and highlight. Optimized microbenchmarks over 1,024 pointer positions measured 30.836 → 1.865 ms with 72 sectors and 317.754 → 25.652 ms at the 768-sector cap (16.5× and 12.4× faster). Exact path containment, including boundaries and gaps, is preserved.

Leaving the map skips list scanning; a single related row scrolls directly to its identifier. Group relationship results survive leaving and re-entering a segment. The cache retains immutable member arrays and compares their storage identity in bounded buffer scopes, invalidating on changed members, map revisions, navigation and filtering. Re-entering a synthetic 100,000-member group measured 146.67 → 0.0018 ms median; first-entry relationship work remains unchanged. These measurements cover specific calculations, not whole-app frame rate or input-to-display latency.


## Public updates (0.9.0)

Sparkle 2.10.1 is pinned in SwiftPM and bundled with the app. The public GitHub latest-release asset serves the appcast; downloads require no GitHub account or user token. Only update metadata and app downloads use the network. Scan contents and indexed paths are not included in update requests. Sparkle checks daily while the app is running, and the user chooses installation and restart. [Sparkle setup](https://sparkle-project.org/documentation/) and [publishing](https://sparkle-project.org/documentation/publishing/).

Both the appcast and universal ZIP carry Ed25519 signatures. The public verification key is embedded in the app; the private key belongs only in the maintainer's Keychain and release secret. Signed-feed validation is required with no expiration fallback, and the archive is verified before extraction. Tag releases build both arm64 and x86_64 for macOS 14, verify signatures and metadata, upload all assets to a draft, then publish it. Release validation rejects mismatched versions, non-increasing build numbers, incomplete Apple credentials, and an unexpected archive URL or length.

The updater defers checks and restart during file removal. An application termination guard also covers Sparkle's resumed-install paths. Ordinary restarts retain saved SQLite maps but clear the session-only collection. The native installer and its helpers perform replacement and relaunch; the application does not implement its own shell-based installer. [Sparkle updater delegate](https://sparkle-project.org/documentation/api-reference/Protocols/SPUUpdaterDelegate.html).

Current releases use ad-hoc code signatures because no Apple Developer ID is available. Sparkle's release signatures authenticate later updates independently; initial internet downloads still require macOS approval. The optional Developer ID path signs nested code, submits it for notarization, staples the result, and only then creates the signed update archive. That path requires credentials and has not been exercised against Apple's service. See RELEASING.md.

Orbit Disk separates filesystem work from searching. A background scan reads names and size metadata, builds a folder tree, and totals sizes from the leaves upward. The result is persisted in a local SQLite index. Opening a saved folder loads its direct children and a bounded preview of larger descendants. Name search reads indexed metadata without reopening files. File contents are never stored in the index.

This follows the useful part of instant-search tools: gather metadata once, then query an index. Everything keeps its running database in memory and indexes size information for fast searches and sorting. Its NTFS change-journal approach belongs to Windows; it is not an API available to this macOS app. [Everything indexes](https://www.voidtools.com/en-us/support/everything/indexes/) and [Everything FAQ](https://www.voidtools.com/faq/).

FSearch is another reference for keeping database construction away from queries. Its source keeps the old index when a replacement scan is cancelled. Its current monitoring documentation also describes the need to rescan at launch and the limitations of filesystem notifications. These are architecture references; Orbit Disk does not incorporate either project's source. [FSearch database implementation](https://github.com/cboxdoerfer/fsearch/blob/master/src/fsearch_database.c) and [database update documentation](https://github.com/cboxdoerfer/fsearch/wiki/Database-update).

On macOS, `getattrlistbulk` retrieves metadata for a batch of directory entries per call. Orbit Disk validates returned attributes and packed record bounds, falls back to a fresh `readdir`/`lstat` enumeration when bulk enumeration fails, and does not follow symbolic links. It skips nested mounted volumes and reports inaccessible entries. Allocated bytes for repeated hard links are counted once per device/inode pair. [Apple's getattrlistbulk manual](https://github.com/apple-oss-distributions/xnu/blob/main/bsd/man/man2/getattrlistbulk.2).

The default map shows logical data-fork bytes, matching `st_size`. Allocated bytes describe filesystem-reported storage and are a separate figure. A sum of file sizes is not a promise of space recovered by deletion: APFS clones share storage, snapshots can retain it, directory metadata is not included, and protected files may be inaccessible. [Apple's getattrlist manual](https://github.com/apple-oss-distributions/xnu/blob/main/bsd/man/man2/getattrlist.2) and [About Apple File System](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundation/about-apple-file-system).

Each scanned root has a database named by its SHA-256 path hash. Inventory format 4 stores numeric node and parent identifiers, basenames and device/inode identities. Full paths are stored once per directory; file paths are reconstructed by joining each basename to its parent directory. Directory paths retain an indexed lookup for navigation and bounded subtree operations. A folder view includes every direct child, then up to 2,000 preview descendants across four levels. The app can open a large saved index without decoding its entire tree. Search uses a folded filename column for case- and accent-insensitive literal matching, with a covering size index for result ordering and counts.

Filesystem events drive targeted subtree refreshes. The stored event ID and monitor identity belong to the same committed snapshot. The event checkpoint is captured before a full scan; monitoring then replays events from that checkpoint to reconcile changes made during scanning. Dropped or invalid event history requires a full refresh. The app verifies the saved monitor/volume identity before reusing its event history. Watcher setup and teardown run off the main thread because macOS may wait for folder-access consent while opening watched ancestors. Events arriving before attachment are buffered, and cancelled startup cannot attach a stale watcher. [Apple's FSEvents guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Darwin/Conceptual/FSEvents_ProgGuide/UsingtheFSEventsFramework/UsingtheFSEventsFramework.html).

Every save or incremental update is prepared in a separate database and published by an atomic rename after its transaction completes. Updates first attempt an APFS file clone, then fall back to copying on other filesystems. A shared mutation lock serializes publication, and revision checks reject stale incremental updates. Cancellation, scan failure and partial incremental scans preserve the previous rows and event watermark. Incremental updates recompute affected folder totals and reassign allocated bytes across all paths sharing a file identity, including when a formerly single-link file gains another link outside the changed subtree. SQLite supplies the transaction mechanism. [SQLite atomic commits](https://www.sqlite.org/atomiccommit.html).

Permission limits remain visible. A full scan may save accessible items with warnings. An incremental scan that encounters inaccessible entries is rejected so it cannot turn missing permissions into apparent deletions. The saved map remains available; resolve folder access and perform a full refresh. Earlier partial-scan warnings remain until a complete refresh replaces them.

Verification should separate first-observed and warm filesystem runs, report file count, elapsed time and peak memory, and avoid promising an instant first scan. Use a generated wide tree of many small files and a nested tree, compare logical sizes with an independent `lstat` walk, and include sparse files, hard links, symlink loops, Unicode names, unavailable paths and cancellation. A forced fallback run should match bulk results. Large-index search latency is a separate measurement from disk scan throughput.

Observed on 26 October 2026 with a release build on this Apple M5 Mac, macOS 27.0.1: scanning a generated fixture with 20,006 files and 102 directories took 148 ms, and the initial index save took 272 ms. Across 20 subsequent operations, cached root loading had a median of 12.7 ms and filename search a median of 4.67 ms. Scanning and updating one 200-file branch took 29.4 ms. The cache occupied 9.81 MB, reduced from 21.09 MB by removing repeated path data from secondary indexes. The scan matched an independent Python `lstat` walk: 88,077,752 logical bytes and 65,576,960 allocated bytes with hard-link deduplication. Cleanup uses the macOS Trash API or `FileManager.removeItem` after the user reviews and confirms the chosen action. Permanent deletion has a separate confirmation and bypasses Trash. ## Progressive mapping (0.8.0, historical)

The original progressive scanner exposed a browsable in-memory preview. Root metadata appeared before directory enumeration; each successful listing exposed discovered files and pending folders before descending into their subtrees. `pending` means traversal or allocation accounting is outstanding, `scanning` means folder totals are still growing, and `complete` means the scan attempt finished. Inaccessible entries retain their separate accessibility flag. Logical bytes, deduplicated allocated bytes, and file counts propagate through ancestors as work completes. Pending folders remain openable, and incomplete sizes are labeled as measured so far.

`DiskScanPreview` stores shallow metadata and child identifiers behind a lock. A view contains every direct child of the displayed folder, plus at most 2,000 deeper nodes across four levels; other ancestors carry totals without unrelated child rows. Size ordering is cached per folder, with name ties. New listings and changes to child logical sizes invalidate the affected ordering caches. The app checks revisions and hydrates views off the main thread, including acquiring the preview lock. Repeated polling skips unchanged revisions, and publications wait while a mouse gesture is active.

The original preview did not write an index, and restarting performed a fresh scan. The resumable pipeline described above supersedes that behavior. Switching locations preserves an active mapping session and resumes its view on return. Visible files and folders can be collected and removed while mapping or saving; incomplete folder sizes are marked as measured so far. Removal checks the root and item identities, hides successful removals immediately, and reconciles affected directories against the saved index. Search also covers discovered preview entries while mapping and labels those results as incomplete; it uses the completed index after handoff. Navigation, selection, and collected items survive the final handoff, and a full refresh keeps an existing saved map available. While removals await reconciliation, saved-index searches use connection-local temporary exclusion and ancestor-adjustment tables to keep visible results and counts current; the published index is opened read-only and remains unchanged.

## Removal recovery

Before mutating selected paths, Orbit records their identities in a cache-wide removal journal. A resumed stage or saved index replays intersecting records, reconciles affected parents, and advances its journal cursor only with a fenced snapshot publication. The journal retains its history; it is not compacted. Live operations are checked separately from abandoned intents. On unusually large recovery sets, the map is temporarily withheld while affected parents are reconciled. More than 10,000 distinct affected parents falls back to a full refresh.

Recovery batches checks for unrelated journal operations and yields between batches, without advancing past a live relevant operation. In one synthetic journal-only test, replay of 10,000 unrelated completed operations fell from 3.88 s to 1.58 s. With 30 concurrent test operations, two runs of the revised path measured 18.4 and 4.2 ms at the 95th percentile, versus 87.9 ms in one baseline run. A separate 80-operation fixture with 3,000 items per operation showed similar replay and contention times for both paths. The batch time limit is checked between probes; an individual filesystem or SQLite call can take longer. These small samples do not predict whole-app performance.

Saved-index recovery processes affected parents one at a time in a private working copy and preserves the published snapshot until the final rename. One exceptionally large parent is still scanned into memory as a full subtree. A forced process termination can leave hidden temporary SQLite clones in the cache; automatic cleanup is intentionally omitted because an uncoordinated cleanup could remove another process's active work. Cache cleanup can reclaim those files. Parent identity and canonical path are rechecked immediately before each pathname-based removal; a separate check-to-mutation race remains if another process replaces a parent between those operations.

Changed hard-link paths are streamed from SQLite during incremental updates rather than collected into an array. In two alternating runs over a synthetic 100,000-pair index, peak RSS fell from about 101 MB to 77 MB; update times overlapped at roughly 1.4 s. Cancellation during path processing and a forced exit before publication preserved the previous snapshot, and a retry verified allocation ownership. The set of distinct affected ancestors and the scanned subtree can still consume memory.

The layout harness emits an AppKit warning about a reentrant `NSTableView` operation while SwiftUI updates estimated row heights. The warning also reproduced in a minimal SwiftUI-only list on the same macOS version. Deferring Orbit's table configuration did not remove it; forcing native fixed heights caused measured layout shifts and was not retained. The 42-state layout check passes with zero measured structural shift. This does not establish whether a future macOS version will change the warning's behavior.

Unicode path handling remains under investigation. A composed folder name was displayed in a staged map but could not be opened because Foundation and the stored path used different byte spellings. A separate generated fixture reproduced a stale saved entry after moving a mapped root through a symlink while filesystem history was unavailable. Scratch fixes are not included in the app: their interaction with byte-distinct names, deletion safety, old journal records, and interrupted repair needs further verification.

## Compact filename search and mapping writes

Orbit now prepares a disposable native filename index from its SQLite inventory. The design borrows compact record storage and candidate filtering ideas from [fsearch](https://github.com/noahdunnagan/fsearch/tree/88e63fdf850cf7749deae8198b187accd21f8ba5) and [EverythingMac](https://github.com/seedds/EverythingMac/tree/c6b657f4f59c90d1e6b8cc0d64ac81d8cac41bd8). It is implemented in Swift; neither project's crawler or ranking engine is imported. Search preparation reads indexed names and IDs, never file contents or a second filesystem traversal.

Folded UTF-8 names live in a byte arena with offsets and row IDs. Hashed three-byte sequences identify candidate blocks of 128 records; exact byte matching rejects hash collisions. Selective queries hydrate only matching IDs from SQLite. Broad saved searches use the cached count and the database's size ordering, preserving global ordering before the result limit. Literal punctuation, embedded NUL queries and Unicode folding retain exact matching semantics.

Unfinished maps use a cached prefix plus a live SQLite tail. New stages allocate monotonically increasing row IDs; a name-generation trigger invalidates a prefix if an existing row's search name changes. The reader captures its prefix before opening a read transaction, then checks its generation and reads current metadata, removals and scan state in that transaction. Deleted cached IDs are harmless: SQLite filters them. Every row beyond the prefix remains searchable. Stages without the necessary schema use the existing SQL path.

Broad staged queries with varied integer sizes aggregate the complete count and validate numeric fields in SQL, then hydrate only the leading size range. Every tie at the result boundary is still examined using the existing normalized path ordering. Equal-size workloads, removal overlays and unusual size representations retain the existing path; a broad query over millions of equal-size files can therefore remain expensive.

The process retains up to 128 MiB of estimated cache storage and admits one builder at a time, with a 96 MiB estimate per index. A capped index retains its useful prefix and searches the remaining SQL tail; saturated stage prefixes are not rebuilt repeatedly. These limits are not a process RSS guarantee: readers can retain older indexes, and an active builder, SQLite page caches and temporary results consume additional memory. The search cache is rebuilt after relaunch and is never required to resume a map.

Saved maps warm the cache on a utility task after a short initial-display delay. Stages prepare it after 8,192 observed entries, then at bounded growth intervals. Search uses the SQL fallback immediately while preparation runs. Ready caches reduce the input debounce from 100 ms to 16 ms. Snapshot changes and model teardown cancel obsolete preparation; publication suspends new stage preparation so optional readers cannot repeatedly delay the WAL checkpoint.

The durable writer omits duplicate staging copies of path, folded name, identity and symlink state. Its page cache is 16 MiB. The first enumeration batch is still committed immediately; later batches use a 1,024-record target and also flush after 50 ms between enumeration callbacks. This is a cooperative bound, not a deadline on a filesystem or SQLite call. SQLite FULL synchronization, the default WAL checkpoint threshold, completed-directory markers, hard-link ownership and removal fences remain in force. Those writer and filename-cache changes alone did not require rewriting saved indexes. The subsequent compact inventory format in 0.9.7 does require a one-time rebuild, described below.

`check-prepared-search.sh` covers cache/fallback parity, partial caches, live tails, name changes, removals and cancellation. The portable core checks also exercise byte matching, block boundaries and memory/candidate limits on the Linux CI job. Existing stage, crash-resume, subtree-reuse and search-lifetime checks remain required.

On Apple M5 with Swift 6.3.3, five warm query rounds over one million synthetic SQLite file records measured saved selective search at a median 70.49 ms without the cache and 0.53 ms with it; absent searches fell from 32.90 to 0.10 ms. A query matching 999,900 records fell from 31.72 to 3.95 ms. Live-stage selective search fell from 66.07 to 1.92 ms, and broad search from 335.25 to 130.35 ms. Counts and ordered IDs matched the SQL reference, including a subsequent 1,000-row uncached tail. Cache construction took 1.78 seconds for the saved fixture and 178 ms for the live fixture in the final run; query warmup does not warm every page needed for construction. Estimated retained cache capacity was 85.72 MiB. These are backend timings, excluding input delay and rendering.

Four alternating fresh-process initial-mapping runs per variant, after a warmup, measured a 100,000-file flat directory at a median 3.200 seconds before and 2.580 seconds after the writer/cache changes. Its database shrank from 121.0 to 99.7 MB, with first readable backend results remaining about 17 ms. One candidate COMMIT stalled for 2.26 seconds; including it, the arithmetic mean improved only 1.5%, versus the 19.4% median improvement. The 10,000-file/1,000-directory and 2,000-empty-directory fixtures were effectively unchanged. The candidate includes progressive cache preparation. These warm synthetic fixtures do not measure incremental refresh, whole-Mac mapping, UI frame latency or a reliable bound on disk stalls.


## Compact durable inventory (0.9.7)

Inventory format 4 stores one basename and parent ID per file, numeric device/inode identities, and full paths only in `directory_paths`. It removes the earlier per-file full path, staging parent/name, duplicate size/allocation/type fields and textual identity. Hard-link owners and candidates refer to node IDs. The app keeps SQLite as the authoritative inventory; no external crawler, search database or raw-volume reader was introduced. Native metadata discovery remains unchanged.

Folder and file lookups reconstruct only the requested rows through an indexed parent join. Saved refreshes materialize their deletion IDs before directory-path triggers run. Staged pruning removes at most 128 rows per transaction, in reverse parent-path order, keeping a surviving descendant's path available across interruption. Its range query uses the directory-path and parent/name indexes without a subtree-wide temporary sort. File identity integers preserve signed device values and the complete UInt64 inode bit pattern.

The same commit boundaries, FULL synchronization, hard-link ownership, removal fences, queue epochs and publication checks remain in place. Old saved inventories are retained until their replacement is publishable. Old-format unfinished stages use a different versioned key and are not resumed; format-4 stages resume normally. Older saved and partial maps therefore rebuild once. Old partial stage files are not automatically deleted, since another app process may still own them. The separate removal journal stays at format 3 and retains pending operations and history.

A saved file's basename follows the existing normalized saved-path representation; search folding uses the observed filename, and literal removal lookups preserve path bytes. Real APFS NFC and NFD fixture checks cover scan, save, lookup and subtree refresh. A reuse listing falls back to filesystem enumeration if folding a stored basename would change its saved search spelling, including some Japanese kana. This does not resolve the separately documented raw-stage Unicode navigation limitation.
